War system proposal · Affairs and Order

The War Board

Every war is played on a hex board built from the two nations' biomes, from a tight 11 by 11 skirmish up to 25 by 25, or on open sea for full naval battles. Your real provinces become territories, your real units become pieces, and every piece moves once per hour.

Try a war

You command Aurelia (blue, west). Click a piece, click a tinted hex to move it, click a red-ringed enemy to attack, or pick an order. Deploy pieces from your reserves with production points, or train more. Skip an hour lets Kharzun answer and moves the clock on. Take their capital to win. Select a piece to see how many real units it stands for, or give its type your own picture. Red hexes show where the selected piece can fire from where it stands.

How turns work in the real game: a turn is one real hour, on the clock. Every hour you get fresh moves whether or not the other player has moved, so you never wait for anyone and nobody can stall a war by not playing. The Skip an hour button exists only in this demo so you can test without waiting a real hour between moves.

Battle log

Your army

The units you already own

Every piece on the board is a unit that exists in the game today: soldiers, tanks, artillery, bombers, fighters, apaches, destroyers, cruisers, submarines, aircraft carriers and spies, plus the newer kamikaze drones, cruise missiles and SAM batteries. ICBMs and nukes are strategic strikes you launch from a stockpile. The counters use the game's own combat rules, so a player who knows the game already knows the board. Five more unit types are marked NEW: engineers, marines, mechanized infantry, rocket artillery and supply convoys. They are proposals the vote can add or drop.

Who beats who

Counters come from the existing rules: tanks beat soldiers, artillery beats tanks, soldiers beat artillery and apaches, fighters beat bombers and apaches, bombers beat tanks, soldiers and ships, apaches beat soldiers and tanks, destroyers beat submarines, cruisers beat destroyers and shoot down air, submarines beat cruisers. Carriers extend air range.

What terrain costs to cross

Terrain decides how far ground pieces go. Every hex costs movement points, so tanks cross 5 plains hexes but only 1 forest hex, and artillery crawls. Ships and aircraft ignore land and simply move a fixed number of hexes. A dash means the piece can never enter that terrain. Rain, storms and snow lower movement, so this table is the clear-weather baseline. A piece with full movement can always make at least one step onto terrain it can enter.

Reserves

Own it, train it, then deploy it

The reserve

Your reserve is the army your nation actually owns. At the start of a war each side has a set of units waiting off the board. In the real game these would be your real unit counts, scaled to fit.

Deploying

Putting a piece on the field costs production points and can only draw from the reserve. No reserve, no deployment, however many points you have. Deployed pieces arrive at your territory or a port and can act next hour.

Choose where to deploy

Press Deploy and every legal hex lights up green: near your towns, or beside any forward base you have built. Pushing deep into enemy land? Build a forward base on the front line and reinforce from there, instead of walking units across the whole map.

Forward bases are objectives

A forward base is a supply source and a deployment point, and enemy land pieces can capture it. Taking the enemy base nearest the front cuts their supply and stops them replacing losses there.

Training

New units are bought with production points and arrive in the reserve after a delay: 1 turn for drones, 2 to 3 for ground units, 4 for most aircraft and ships, 6 for a carrier. ICBMs take 3 turns and nukes 5.

Losses are permanent

A destroyed piece does not return to the reserve. Wars drain what you own, so a long war is decided by who can train faster than they lose pieces.

Army size

How a big army fits on the board

One piece, many units

The board never shows 16 million soldiers. A piece stands for an equal share of what you own. With 16 million soldiers and 16 pieces, each piece is 1 million men and its strength bar is scaled to match. Select a piece in the demo to see what it represents and how much of it is left.

Casualties in real numbers

The bar is a percentage, but losses are reported in real units. A hit that takes 18% off a million-man piece reads as about 180,000 soldiers lost in the battle log, and your army totals drop with it.

Bigger army, bigger pieces

Pieces from a larger army represent more units, so they last longer and hit harder than pieces from a small army, with diminishing returns so size matters without deciding everything. Small nations get small pieces, not fewer of them.

Pick your battlefield

Choose the board size that suits you: Skirmish 11x11 (12 pieces a side), Small 15x15 (16), Medium 19x19 (24) or Large 25x25 (32), and override the field limit up to 64. Smaller boards put the armies in contact fast, so you get a real front line instead of scattered forts. The real game would size the board from the armies involved.

Your own pictures

Pieces would use the unit pictures you picked in the game instead of the default symbols. In the demo, select a piece and press "Use my own picture" to try it. The image stays in your browser and is never uploaded.

Air, sea and missiles

Three extra layers

Air bases and carriers

Fighters and bombers can operate within 9 hexes of one of your settlements or aircraft carriers, apaches within 5. Lose the base and the aircraft run out of fuel and take damage each turn. Sink the carrier and every plane near it is stranded. Aircraft next to a base or carrier also repair.

Submarines and the deep

Submarines are invisible unless an enemy destroyer is within 3 hexes, a cruiser or aircraft within 2, or a spy within 3. Destroyers hit them 60% harder, and bombers also find them. A hidden submarine hits cruisers and carriers hard.

Drones, missiles and SAMs

Kamikaze drones and cruise missiles fly in, hit once and are used up. Each SAM battery within range gives a chance to shoot them down, and SAMs shield nearby friends from all air attacks by 35%. Fighters and cruisers can also hit aircraft in the air.

ICBMs and nukes

Buy them from production points and launch at any hex, even one you cannot see. An ICBM hits a hex and its 6 neighbors, a nuke 19 hexes, leaving fallout for 6 turns. SAM batteries near the target can intercept, and friendly pieces in the blast are hit too. This is where the Iron Dome and Star Wars projects plug in.

Fair terrain

With Fair terrain on (the default) the generator evens out how hard each half of the board is to march across, so neither side gets an easy desert while the other fights through forest and hills. The battle log shows the average march cost for each side.

Naval wars

Some boards, or any board you pick, are open sea with island ports, for full naval battles. Ships and aircraft carry the war. Park a surface ship beside an enemy port with none of their pieces next to it for 3 turns and the port is blockaded and taken. Tiny ponds never appear, so ships are never stranded.

Generation

Biomes build the battlefield

Each nation has a biome. The generator rolls one smooth noise field over the board, then turns it into terrain using the mix of the nation that owns that side. Around the middle columns the two mixes blend, so the front line is always a meeting of both homelands. A seed makes a board reproducible, so a war can be replayed or spectated from the same map.

Terrain order runs low to high (sea, swamp, plains, desert, tundra, forest, hills, mountain), so water sits next to marsh and peaks sit next to hills. If the two capitals are cut apart by sea, a land bridge surfaces between them.

Provinces

Your provinces are the map

Real names

Each province you own becomes a territory with its own name and a settlement at its heart. A Nordvik province in an arctic nation is named Nordvik on the board, and its border is drawn on the map.

Capture

End a turn with a piece on an enemy settlement and the whole province flips to you. The capital is the last settlement standing. Take it and the war is won on the board.

Income

Every province you hold pays production points each turn, so a flipped province moves income from one side to the other. Production points pay to deploy units from your reserves and to train new ones.

Tiles

Every terrain does something

Terrain is alive on the board: trees sway, desert air shimmers, snow falls on tundra, swamp bubbles, surf foams on every coast and settlements puff chimney smoke. Each one also changes the rules.

Battlefield memory

Fights leave scorched craters that stay on the map. Destroyed pieces leave smoking wrecks for three hours. Forts, depots and mines are drawn on the hex that holds them, and a fort can be knocked down a level by artillery, bombers, ships or missiles.

Zones of control

A ground or sea piece that steps next to an enemy must stop there. Enemies cannot be walked past, so a line of pieces holds a front and tanks cannot simply run through a gap. Aircraft, drones and missiles ignore it.

Hidden pieces

Land pieces in forest and swamp are invisible unless an enemy stands next to them or a spy is within 3 hexes. Hidden units also get a first-strike ambush bonus, so spies matter.

Time and weather

The clock and the sky

Turn 1 is 06:00. Night runs 21:00 to 04:00 and cuts vision by 1. Weather changes on its own, always shows a one-hour forecast, and each type changes what pieces can do.

Each biome has its own weather table. Deserts get sandstorms, arctic nations get snow and blizzards, islands and tropics get storms. A war between two biomes mixes both tables.

Depth

What happens inside a fight

Damage = Attack × (0.4 + 0.6×HP%) × (0.7 + 0.3×Org%) × Matchup × (1 − Cover) × Flank × Supply × Veteran × Weather × Terrain × SAM cover × Commander × Fortune Cover = Terrain + 0.18×Fort + 0.10×Dig + Commander (cap 0.70, aircraft 0) Flank = 1 + 0.20 per other friendly piece touching target (max +40%) Veteran = 1 + 0.08 per level (0 to 3) SAM = ×0.65 against air attacks if an enemy SAM battery is within 2 hexes Org hit = Damage × 1.25 (×1.3 if flanked, ×1.4 from artillery, bombers, ships, missiles)

Organization and rout

Every piece has Strength and Organization. Hits drain both. At 0 Org the piece routs, falls back 2 hexes, loses a turn to regroup and takes extra damage if chased by tanks, spies or aircraft. A piece with nowhere to run is trapped and badly hurt. Flanks and artillery break Org fastest.

Veterans

Pieces earn experience from attacks, kills and surviving counter-fire. Three levels (shown as stars) give +8% each. Reinforcements arrive green, so keeping veterans alive beats feeding fresh bodies into the line.

Spies

Spies cannot fight but see 5 hexes, spot hidden pieces and submarines, and sabotage adjacent forts, depots, mines or pieces. They are caught about half the time they fail, which ties into the existing espionage system.

Commanders

Each side draws a commander trait: Master Builder (forts, depots and mines take one turn less), Armor Pioneer (+10% attack and +1 move for tanks), Fortress Builder (+6% cover), Quartermaster (+2 supply range, faster Org recovery) or Spymaster (+1 vision).

Worked example: tanks (100 HP, 100 Org) hit soldiers on hills with a level 1 fort and 2 dig levels, with one flanker. Cover = 0.30 + 0.18 + 0.20 = 0.68, so damage is 34 × 1.4 × 0.32 × 1.2 ≈ 18. The soldiers hit back for roughly 5. Fortified hills laugh at tanks, so bring artillery (it beats tanks and soldiers alike), bombers or a flank.

Satellites

Train a satellite (35 PP, 4 turns) and it goes into orbit. Once per 4 turns you can run a scan: the whole map is visible for that turn and the next, so you can watch enemy armies move. Canopy hides what is under it, so pieces in forest and swamp, and submarines, stay hidden unless a spy or ship is close.

Movement trails

Every piece that moved in the last hour leaves a dashed arrow showing where it came from. You see the trails of anything you can see, so a satellite scan or an aerial surveillance pass shows how the whole enemy force is shifting, and tells you where the push is coming.

Turn structure

One hour, step by step

Turns run on the real clock, not on a button. Every hour each side gets a fresh set of moves, whatever the other player does. During the hour you queue orders, they lock at :55, and everything then resolves together in the same fixed order. A replay plays for everyone, spectators included. It stays chess-like, but because both sides move at once you are always guessing what the other side is doing. The demo above lets you move instantly and skip hours so you can feel the board.

Can someone stall a war by not playing?

No. The hour passes whether you move or not. If you skip an hour, your pieces hold where they are, orders already running (forts, depots, mines, training) keep going, and the enemy keeps moving. Skipping only hurts the one who skips.

Do I wait for the other player?

Never. You move, and one hour later you can move again, regardless of what the other player has done. Both sides give orders during the same hour and they resolve together.

What if I am asleep or offline?

Part of the proposal: standing orders such as Hold, Dig in or Fall back, so an army is never helpless while you are away. Anything you set keeps working until you change it.

What is the button in the demo?

Demo only. Skip an hour lets you test the board without waiting a real hour for every move. The real game has no such button.
